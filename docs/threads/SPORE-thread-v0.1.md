## § 0 · The scope boundary, declared first

**SPORE is the culinary and medicinal mushroom cultivation practice.** The catalogue covers gourmet edibles (oyster, lion's mane, shiitake, chestnut, pioppino, blue oyster, king trumpet) and traditional medicinal species (reishi, turkey tail, cordyceps militaris). 

**The catalogue does not include and will never include psychoactive species** — *Psilocybe cubensis* and related genera are controlled substances under Canada's Controlled Drugs and Substances Act and are explicitly out of scope. The studio's practice does not enter that regulatory grey zone, period. Any future shifts in Canadian psilocybin policy do not change this — SPORE is gourmet and medicinal only.

This boundary matters because a significant portion of the publicly available CVG / shoebox cultivation literature is written for psilocybin cultivation. The technique is the same; the species are different; the regulatory category is night-and-day. The studio's SPORE materials must be unambiguously culinary in their imagery, copy, and supplier relationships.

## § 1 · What SPORE is

The fifth M1ND.studio practice, completing the indoor microfarm architecture alongside BDRC (grain meals) and GROW (hydroponic herbs and greens). Where GROW produces leafy crops via the iDOO appliance and BDRC produces grain meals via the rice cooker, SPORE produces fresh culinary mushrooms via the standardized mason-jar-and-shoebox-tote workflow.

The thesis is consistent with the rest of the catalogue: **commodity envelopes + studio curation = a real product line where the studio earns margin through editorial work, not through manufacturing.** The envelopes here are 1L mason jars (for grain spawn) and standard shoebox-sized plastic totes (for fruiting). Both are sourced anywhere — Canadian Tire, Walmart, hardware stores, restaurant supply. The studio designs the contents and the workflow.

### What's different about SPORE versus GROW and BDRC

| Practice | Active cycle | Equipment cost | Customer experience |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BDRC** | 25 minutes per meal | $25 (rice cooker) | Press toggle, eat |
| **GROW** | 6-12 week harvest cycles, daily water check | $75-105 (iDOO) | Plant, watch, harvest |
| **SPORE** | **3-6 week colonization + 2-3 week fruiting** | **$15-25 total in totes + jars** | **Inoculate, wait, mist, harvest** |

SPORE has the **lowest hardware cost** of any indoor production practice in the catalogue — total appliance equivalent is under $30 because the "appliance" is just jars and totes, which most households already have versions of. The customer's monetary commitment is almost entirely in the cells the studio ships.

The customer's *time* commitment is also different. GROW asks for daily water-level checks and weekly nutrient additions. SPORE asks for 30 seconds of misting twice a day during the 2-week fruiting phase, and almost nothing during the 3-6 week colonization phase. **It's the lowest-attention live practice in the catalogue** once a cell is going.

## § 2 · The standardized workflow

Field-tested in Squamish over 2024-2025. Commercialization begins Toronto summer 2026.

### 2.1 The two physical envelopes

**MJ1L · the 1 Litre mason jar (grain spawn vessel).** Standard wide-mouth 1L Bernardin/Mason jar with a modified lid. Lid modification: **a 1/2" hole drilled through the metal lid, fitted with a synthetic filter disc (Tyvek or polyfill) and a self-healing injection port**. This is the canonical preparation for sterile-environment grain spawn cultivation, used by amateur and professional growers worldwide. The studio sources jars from Canadian Tire / Home Hardware ($2-3/jar). Lid modification is a one-time setup using a 1/2" hole punch and adhesive filter discs ($0.50/jar in materials).

**SBT · the shoebox tote (fruiting chamber).** Standard ~6L clear plastic shoebox tote (Sterilite, IRIS, or generic). Lid drilled with ~12-16 quarter-inch holes around the upper sides for fresh air exchange (FAE), then taped with micropore tape until colonization is complete. This is the canonical "monotub" architecture scaled down to apartment-friendly dimensions. **The tote is the fruiting chamber and the lid is the humidity manager.** Sourced from Canadian Tire / Walmart / Dollarama at $5-10/tote.

These two envelopes are the entire physical infrastructure. **No grow tents, no ultrasonic foggers, no expensive flow hoods, no laminar workbenches.** The studio's practice deliberately stays at the apartment-friendly end of the technique spectrum — the same equipment a serious home cook or fermenter would already have, or could acquire for under $30.

### 2.2 The workflow, in studio terms

```
SPORE workflow · canonical cycle

Stage 1: SPAWN PREPARATION (Week 1)
  └─ 1L mason jar (MJ1L) loaded with sterilized whole-oat/rye/millet grain
  └─ Inoculated with liquid culture or grain transfer
  └─ Incubated at 22-27°C for 14-21 days until fully colonized
  └─ Result: a jar of fully white-mycelium-permeated grain

Stage 2: BULK SUBSTRATE (Week 3)
  └─ Coco coir + vermiculite + gypsum (CVG) prepared
  └─ Pasteurized via boiling water (no pressure cooker needed for CVG)
  └─ Cooled to room temperature in sealed bucket (4-8 hours)
  └─ Result: ~2L of moist, sterile bulk substrate at field capacity

Stage 3: SPAWN-TO-BULK (Week 3, same day as substrate prep)
  └─ Colonized grain spawn from MJ1L broken up and mixed into CVG
  └─ Spawn-to-bulk ratio: 1:4 to 1:9 by volume
  └─ Inoculated substrate transferred to SBT shoebox tote
  └─ Lid taped, placed in incubation at 22-27°C for 10-14 days

Stage 4: PINNING + FRUITING (Week 5-6)
  └─ Tote opens, tape removed from FAE holes
  └─ Twice-daily misting with spray bottle
  └─ Pin formation within 3-5 days
  └─ Harvest 7-14 days later
  └─ Result: 100-400g fresh mushrooms per tote depending on species

Stage 5: SECOND FLUSH (Week 7-8, optional)
  └─ After first harvest, "dunk" the substrate cake in water 2-4 hours
  └─ Return to fruiting chamber
  └─ Second flush in 7-10 days
  └─ Typical yield: 50-70% of first flush
```

A complete cycle from inoculation to final harvest runs ~6-8 weeks. The customer's active time across the entire cycle is approximately **45 minutes total** — most of which is in Stages 2 and 3 (substrate prep and spawn-to-bulk). The rest is checking on the cycle and twice-daily misting in Stage 4.

This is meaningfully less work than gardening, less mess than a kitchen herb pot, and produces more mushrooms than most Toronto households actually eat. **One successful SBT yields enough for 3-4 dinners.**

## § 3 · The SPORE cell · what the studio designs

Like BDRC and GROW, the SPORE practice productizes the workflow into 1646-compatible cells. The cell is what the customer buys; the studio designs what's inside.

### 3.1 Two cell formats

**SPORE Cell · Type A · "Spawn cell"** — for customers who already have CVG substrate or who buy it separately. Contains:
- 1× pre-inoculated MJ1L jar of grain spawn (fully colonized, ready to mix into bulk substrate)
- Strain identity card (Mode A image-led, species + cultivar + provenance)
- Care card (Mode B, what to do with this jar — when to mix to bulk, target temperatures)
- 3-card recipe deck for cooking the eventual harvest

**SPORE Cell · Type B · "Complete starter cell"** — for first-time growers, the full kit. Contains:
- 1× pre-inoculated MJ1L grain spawn (same as Type A)
- 1× CVG dry-mix packet (500g coco coir + 500g vermiculite + 100g gypsum) — customer adds boiling water
- Filter disc + injection port hardware for one more MJ1L (customer recycles a jar)
- Pair of nitrile gloves for sterile work
- Spray bottle (small, for misting)
- 7-card recipe deck (Mode A frontispiece + Mode B substrate prep + Mode B spawn-to-bulk + Mode B fruiting care + Mode A harvest + Mode A cooking suggestions + Mode B compliance)

Type A cells: ~$28-36 retail (target). Type B cells: ~$55-75 retail (target). **The Type B cell is the catalogue's introductory product** because most customers entering the practice need the full kit; experienced growers can switch to Type A refills.

### 3.2 Why this is the right cell architecture

The SPORE cell ships **at the moment immediately before bulk substrate inoculation.** The studio handles the difficult, contamination-sensitive part of the workflow (sterile-environment grain spawn cultivation) in its Toronto facility, and ships customers a stable, fully-colonized jar that's robust to handling. The customer handles only the relatively forgiving parts of the workflow: substrate prep (pasteurization, not sterilization — much more tolerant of mistakes) and fruiting management.

**This division of labor maps directly to where commercial mushroom cultivation already draws its line.** Commercial substrate-and-spawn suppliers (North Spore in Maine, Fungi Perfecti in Washington, Smug Bug here in Ontario) follow the same model: they prepare colonized spawn in clean-lab conditions and ship it; customers handle the bulk-substrate step and fruiting at home. **The studio's SPORE practice is the same model with editorial design and 1646 catalogue integration on top.**

## § 4 · The proposed 12 SPORE cells

Mirroring the GROW catalogue at 12 cells. Each cell is one species/cultivar at production-ready specification.

### Group 1 · Beginner-friendly oysters (Cells 01-04)

The most forgiving cells. The species most tolerant of suboptimal conditions, fastest to fruit, highest yield per substrate volume. Where every SPORE customer starts.

**Cell 01 · Blue Oyster** *(Pleurotus ostreatus)*
The catalogue's default first cell. Fast (5-week cycle), reliable, beautiful (the blue-grey color photographs well), and forgiving of temperature variation. Yields 300-500g per SBT first flush. **The cell most customers buy first.**

**Cell 02 · Yellow / Golden Oyster** *(Pleurotus citrinopileatus)*
Visually striking, distinctive butter-and-cashew flavor, slightly more delicate than blue oysters. Yields slightly lower (200-350g) but presents better as a finished dish. **The photogenic cell.**

**Cell 03 · Pink Oyster** *(Pleurotus djamor)*
The "ham-flavored" oyster. Vibrant pink color, meatier texture, less aroma than blue/yellow. Shorter shelf life (3-4 days post-harvest) so best for immediate use. **The novelty cell that surprises every first-time grower.**

**Cell 04 · Phoenix Oyster (Italian Oyster)** *(Pleurotus pulmonarius)*
The summer-tolerant oyster — fruits well at temperatures up to 30°C where blue oysters struggle. Important for Toronto summers without dedicated cooling. **The seasonal cell.**

### Group 2 · Gourmet specialty (Cells 05-08)

The more advanced cells — longer cycles, more specific substrate or environmental needs, but the species that justify SPORE as a serious practice.

**Cell 05 · Lion's Mane** *(Hericium erinaceus)*
The catalogue's most-photographed-by-customers cell. The white pom-pom fruit bodies are unmistakable. Crab/lobster-adjacent flavor. Slightly longer cycle (7-8 weeks). Yields 200-400g. **The cell that converts skeptics — anyone who's never grown a mushroom is fascinated by lion's mane.**

**Cell 06 · King Trumpet (King Oyster)** *(Pleurotus eryngii)*
The meatiest mushroom in the catalogue. Dense, hearty, the closest analog to a vegetable steak. Cycle 6-7 weeks. Yields 200-300g of large, sliceable fruit bodies. **The cell for serious cooks.**

**Cell 07 · Chestnut Mushroom** *(Pholiota adiposa)*
Cluster-fruiting, nutty flavor, somewhat closer to shiitake than to oysters in texture. Cycle 6-8 weeks. Yields 200-350g. **The cell that diversifies a customer's catalogue beyond oysters.**

**Cell 08 · Pioppino (Black Poplar Mushroom)** *(Cyclocybe aegerita)*
Italian gourmet species, brown-capped, complex earthy flavor. Cycle 7-9 weeks. Yields 150-300g. Less common, more interesting, sought by serious cooks. **The cell that demonstrates the catalogue's depth.**

### Group 3 · Medicinal (Cells 09-10)

The non-edible-but-extractable cells. Different value proposition: customers don't cook these directly; they're dried, powdered, and consumed as tea, tincture, or capsule. Genuinely a different product than Group 1-2.

**Cell 09 · Reishi (Lingzhi)** *(Ganoderma lucidum)*
The most-traditional Chinese medicinal mushroom. Hard, woody fruit bodies, bitter taste, dried for tea or tincture. Long cycle (10-16 weeks). Yields 100-200g dry. **The patient-grower cell.**

**Cell 10 · Turkey Tail** *(Trametes versicolor)*
Bracket fungus, beautiful concentric color bands, supported by significant immunomodulatory research. Dried and powdered for capsules or tea. Cycle 8-12 weeks. Yields 50-100g dry. **The cell with the strongest functional-mushroom market positioning.**

### Group 4 · Specialty / advanced (Cells 11-12)

**Cell 11 · Shiitake on supplemented sawdust** *(Lentinula edodes)*
The catalogue's only cell that requires a different substrate (hardwood sawdust + bran rather than CVG). Worth including because shiitake is the most-recognized mushroom in the catalogue and most Canadians have eaten it. Cycle 8-10 weeks. Yields 250-400g. **The cell that proves SPORE handles serious species beyond oysters.**

**Cell 12 · Cordyceps Militaris** *(Cordyceps militaris)*
The most exotic cell in the catalogue. Bright orange fruit bodies, traditional functional medicine, requires unique substrate (rice-based with specific supplementation). Cycle 8-12 weeks. Yields 50-100g. **The cell for the catalogue's most adventurous customer.**

### What's deliberately not in the catalogue

- **Psilocybin species** — declared out of scope in § 0
- **Morels, truffles, chanterelles** — these cannot be cultivated reliably on home substrate; the catalogue stays honest about what works
- **Button mushrooms / portobello** — these require composted manure substrate that's smelly, messy, and not apartment-friendly
- **Wild-foraged species kits** — too much regulatory complexity around foraging-related products

### The spawn production economics

The studio's defensible asset is its in-house grain spawn production. Approximate economics:

- **Lab setup:** ~$300 in initial equipment (pressure cooker, glass culture vessels, agar plates, liquid culture syringes)
- **Per-jar production cost:** ~$1.50 in grain, jar, lid, filter, and reagent costs
- **Time per jar:** ~5 minutes of active labor amortized over batch production
- **Contamination rate:** ~5-10% even with good clean technique — built into pricing

The studio can produce roughly 100-200 spawn jars per week from a small dedicated Toronto studio area (~10 sq ft of clean bench plus a shelf for incubation). **At $5 cost-loaded production and $25-65 retail effect across Type A and Type B cells, the studio's spawn production is the practice's most leveraged operational asset.**

## § 6 · CFIA regulatory scoping

Food safety regulation for cultivated mushrooms is genuinely different from herbs and grains, and the studio needs to be careful here.

### What CFIA cares about

Cultivated edible mushrooms shipped or sold in Canada fall under the **Safe Food for Canadians Regulations (SFCR)** and inherit most of the same compliance obligations as the BDRC and STOCK products:

- Food handler permit (provincial — Ontario in Toronto)
- CFIA SFCR license if shipping interprovincially or hitting $30K gross sales
- Labeling compliance — same Nutrition Facts table, allergen, bilingual rules
- Preventive Control Plan (PCP)
- Allergen declarations (mushrooms themselves are not on the priority allergen list, but cross-contact with substrate ingredients could matter — gypsum is mineral, no allergen concern; coco coir no allergen concern; grain substrate inherits grain allergens — **wheat-allergic customers should be cautioned about chestnut and shiitake cells specifically because of their bran content**)

### What's different from BDRC/STOCK

**Mushroom spawn is technically a live biological culture.** Some regulatory categories distinguish between food products and biological cultures. The studio's spawn cells are sold as **"food cultivation kits"** rather than "food" per se — the customer cultivates the mushrooms themselves and consumes their own home-cultivated harvest. This is the same category as commercial mushroom spawn already sold in Canada by North Spore, Fungi Perfecti (US-shipped), Smug Bug Ontario, etc. **Established regulatory precedent exists.**

However: **if the studio ever ships dried or fresh mushrooms directly to customers, those become food products subject to the full SFCR food-handling regime.** SPORE's current model avoids this — the studio ships cultivation kits, customers grow and consume themselves. **Maintaining this boundary is important.**

### Strain provenance and intellectual property

The studio sources spawn cultures from established Canadian mushroom culture libraries:
- North Spore (Maine, USA — ships to Canada): authoritative strains, well-documented
- Aloha Medicinals (Hawaii, USA): cordyceps and other functional species
- Smug Bug Ontario: local culinary strains, Toronto-friendly
- Mycelio (Quebec): francophone Canadian supplier

**The studio does not isolate new strains from foraged specimens.** That's a different regulatory category and would require relationships with academic mycology departments. SPORE works with established, documented, commercially-available cultures only.

## § 7 · The compounding catalogue · SPORE × BDRC × GROW

The studio's catalogue keeps revealing new compounding effects when products live in the same kitchen. SPORE adds significant new ones:

### Fresh mushrooms finish BDRC meals

**BDRC Cell 09 · Mushroom Farro** currently uses dried porcini (which the cell ships). With a customer's own SPORE harvest, the recipe upgrades to *"fresh oyster mushrooms sautéed and folded in at the finish, OR fresh lion's mane shredded and seared as a topping"*. The customer's catalogue gets richer the more practices they own.

Other BDRC cells that benefit from SPORE fresh mushrooms:
- **Cell 05 · Lemon-Dill Pilaf** — sautéed king trumpets on top
- **Cell 07 · Khichdi** — sautéed oysters folded into the porridge
- **Cell 13 · Bibimbap** — fresh oyster mushrooms as a bibimbap topping (canonical Korean preparation)

### SPORE substrate after-life feeds GROW soil amendments

Spent mushroom substrate (the CVG cake after the second flush is done) is **excellent soil amendment** — full of mycelial residue and breakable organic matter. While the iDOO is hydroponic and doesn't use soil, customers with any soil-based plants (outdoor gardens, houseplants, kitchen herbs in pots) benefit from the spent substrate as compost. **Nothing in the catalogue goes to waste.**

A complete demonstrational meal showing all three indoor production practices in one plate:

## § 8 · Risks and honest edges

**Risk 1 · Contamination is real.** Even with sterile-environment grain spawn from the studio, customer-prepared CVG can grow mold or harbor pathogens. The recipe deck must include honest troubleshooting guidance — what to look for, when to discard, how to restart. *Cell-level failure rate at customer hands likely 10-15% even with good instructions.* The studio's policy should be a no-questions replacement on any failed cell within 30 days — protects customer experience, costs roughly $5-7 per replacement (just the spawn jar, customer keeps the original cell).

**Risk 2 · Apartment air quality during fruiting.** Fruiting mushrooms release significant numbers of spores. Most are harmless — most edible mushroom spores are not allergens — but oyster mushroom spores in particular can cause respiratory irritation in sensitive individuals during heavy fruiting. **The recipe deck should recommend harvesting before full spore release**, and note this potential sensitivity. *Worth a clear warning card in every Type B starter cell.*

**Risk 3 · The psilocybin association problem.** Despite the catalogue's strict gourmet-and-medicinal scope, "mushroom kit on the internet" carries cultural associations the studio needs to actively dispel. **Catalogue copy and imagery must lean hard into culinary aesthetic** — chef-coded, food-magazine-coded, restaurant-coded — not the underground-tech-coded register of much current mushroom-growing content. **The studio's editorial register naturally protects against this**, but it requires conscious vigilance.

**Risk 4 · Seasonal temperature variability.** Toronto summers can hit 32°C; winters drop apartments to 18°C if heating is uneven. Mushroom species have temperature preferences. **The catalogue should clearly tag each cell with optimal temperature ranges**, and seasonal release of cells (Phoenix oysters in summer, blue oysters in winter, etc.) is a real operational pattern.

**Risk 5 · The spawn shipping cold-chain question.** Pre-inoculated grain spawn ships at room temperature (mycelium is dormant-but-alive at fridge-to-room-temp range). Winter shipping to colder Canadian provinces could freeze the spawn and kill the culture. **The studio should restrict winter SPORE shipping to insulated packaging and warn customers about transit expectations during cold months.** Same operational constraint as GROW.

**Risk 6 · Customer education curve is real.** Like GROW but more so, SPORE asks more of the customer than BDRC. First-time growers will need email support, possibly video walkthroughs, definitely a robust troubleshooting card in the deck. The catalogue's onboarding investment is higher than for the dry-goods practices.

## § 10 · Open questions for next session

**Q1 · The Squamish-to-Toronto transition.** What practical changes does the Toronto environment require versus the Squamish field tests? Specifically: water quality (Toronto chlorinated municipal — needs dechlorination?), seasonal humidity variation (winter Toronto can be very dry — affects fruiting humidity management), apartment heating consistency.

**Q2 · The spawn-production studio space requirements.** Approximately 10 sq ft of clean bench plus shelf-mounted incubation. **Does this fit in the Dupont Arts studio plan,** or does it need its own dedicated zone with isolated airflow?

**Q3 · Strain sourcing decisions.** North Spore is the gold standard but ships from Maine (cross-border logistics). Smug Bug Ontario is local but smaller selection. **Probably a hybrid — Smug Bug for species they carry, North Spore for the more exotic species.** Worth establishing both supplier relationships in Phase 0.

**Q4 · The price-point validation.** Type B starter at $55-75 is positioned to fit alongside GROW starter cells and BDRC mid-tier pricing. Type A refill at $28-36 sits between BDRC and STOCK refills. **Are these the right anchor points,** or should SPORE skew higher to signal the more advanced/specialized nature of the practice?

**Q5 · Substrate after-life messaging.** The "spent substrate as soil amendment" angle is genuinely useful and reinforces the catalogue's no-waste thesis. **Should the recipe deck include a "what to do with the spent substrate" card,** or is that too much instruction for one cell? My read: include it. It's the kind of catalogue-defining detail that makes SPORE feel different from any commercial alternative.

**Q6 · The compliance card formatting.** Mushroom cells will have a *different* Nutrition Facts table than BDRC cells — the "as sold" product is a cultivation kit, not edible food. The Nutrition Facts apply to the eventual cultivated harvest. **How does this get framed?** Worth a careful regulatory review — possibly the cell ships without a Nutrition Facts table (it's a cultivation kit, not a food) but with a "expected nutrition of cultivated harvest" reference card.

## § 11 · Closing

The fifth practice. Mushroom cultivation at apartment scale, productized through 1646 cells, integrating into the studio's catalogue alongside BDRC, GROW, and STOCK. The thesis pattern holds: **commodity envelopes (mason jars + shoebox totes), studio curation (the spawn library and the recipe decks), no manufacturing of what's already commoditized.**

What SPORE adds to the catalogue beyond a new product line:

- The studio's first **biological-culture practice** — the spawn is alive, the substrate is alive, the customer's interaction is over weeks of patience rather than minutes of cooking
- The strongest **compounding effect with BDRC** — fresh mushrooms finishing toggle-switch grain meals is the catalogue's most defensible meal-quality argument
- The lowest **customer hardware cost** in the entire indoor microfarm system — $30 total for jars and totes
- A genuine **regulatory boundary** that the studio's editorial register naturally protects — keeping SPORE clearly gourmet-and-medicinal in a category that overlaps with grey-zone cultivation

The work from here is bench testing, the spawn-production setup, and the visual mockup work that BDRC and GROW have already mapped out. **Three of the five M1ND.studio practices are now indoor food-production practices** — BDRC, GROW, and SPORE — and they live together on one Wowlive shelf. The cooking practice the studio's been building toward has its full hardware and biological stack.

What remains is the integration story (covered in MICROSYSTEM-thread-v0.1) and the Hako deployment story (next session).
