## § 0 · What this template is

Every MHYC neighborhood profile follows the same structure. The template ensures:
- **Comparability across profiles** — a reader can compare LES to Friedrichshain to Trinity Bellwoods using identical analytical categories
- **Editorial coherence** — the studio's register stays consistent as different writers contribute
- **Matching tool integration** — the structured "Match Notes" section feeds directly into MESH's matching tool corpus
- **Update tractability** — profiles can be refreshed periodically without re-architecting; only the time-sensitive sections update

Length target: **3,500-5,000 words**, longer where the neighborhood warrants it. Register per MHYC-thread-v0.2 § 3.3: *The Architectural Review* sentence-level, *Dezeen* visual, McKinsey analytical, *Failed Architecture* critical.

## § 1 · Template structure

### Header block

```
# [Neighborhood Name], [City]

## [A subtitle naming the specific cultural moment or thesis]

**MHYC.NNNN** · [date published] · [date last reviewed]
**Author:** [name or M1ND.studio · MHYC]
**Coordinates:** [center lat/long for matching tool]
**Boundaries:** [4 streets / natural boundaries / district codes]
**Population (year):** [number] across [number] households
**One-line summary:** [The 15-word answer to "what is this neighborhood?"]
```

### § 1 · The opening provocation (200-400 words)

Lead with what's distinctive — the thing about this neighborhood you couldn't say about any other place. **Not "vibrant and diverse."** Specifically what makes this one this one. The provocation establishes voice and earns the reader's continued attention.

For Trinity Bellwoods, the provocation might be the white squirrel and what it represents. For Lower East Side, it might be the unbroken thread of immigrant succession. For Friedrichshain, it might be the wall-traces and what they mean now. Each profile leads with its specific thing.

### § 2 · The current moment (500-800 words)

What's happening here, right now, in 2026? Who's moving in, who's leaving, what's emerging, what's ending? The dynamic state of the neighborhood at publication date.

Honest about both the good and the friction. Gentrification dynamics, displacement, what's been lost, what's emerging. **Never propaganda — for or against the neighborhood.**

This section gets updated on annual review cycles; it's where the profile shows its date.

### § 3 · The historical context (400-600 words)

How did this neighborhood become what it is? Not exhaustive history — the analytically-load-bearing history. The decisions, migrations, and built environment that explain why the place feels the way it does today.

For Trinity Bellwoods, this is Garrison Creek, Trinity College, the Bay-and-Gable wave, Portuguese immigration, the Ossington liquor-license moratorium. For LES, this is tenements, immigration succession, CBGB, the East Village split. **Choose the threads that explain the present**, not the threads that fill a Wikipedia page.

### § 4 · The practical math (500-800 words)

The genuinely useful information for someone evaluating the neighborhood as a place to live, work, or rotate through:

- **Housing costs** — typical rent ranges by unit type, purchase prices, what's actually available
- **Transit** — connection quality to the rest of the city, walkability, bike infrastructure
- **Climate** — what the seasons feel like *in this specific neighborhood* (microclimate matters)
- **Food access** — daily groceries, specialty importers, restaurants, the food culture
- **Internet & utility infrastructure** — relevant for remote workers
- **Daily-life logistics** — the practical texture of getting through a week here

Numbers should be specific and dated. **No vague "it's pricey" — give the median rent and link to a source.** This section is the McKinsey analytical register applied locally.

### § 5 · The community access points (400-600 words)

Where does a newcomer plug in? This is the hardest section to write well and the most useful when done right.

Specific places, recurring events, public spaces that function as social infrastructure, the businesses where regulars become known, the institutions (libraries, community centers, religious institutions, sports clubs) that scaffold longer-term belonging.

Distinguished from a "where to eat" listicle by **prioritizing places where you can become a regular** over places that are merely good once.

### § 6 · The texture (400-600 words)

The hardest section to fake and the most important for the matching tool. The specific sensory and cultural texture that makes this place feel like this place:

- The specific way people occupy public space
- The rhythms of weekday vs weekend
- The sounds, the smells, the visual register
- The kinds of people you see on the street, in cafés, in the park
- The cultural moments — events, traditions, recurring informal gatherings
- The micro-rituals that locals understand

This section is what enables matching ("Trinity Bellwoods is to Friedrichshain as Williamsburg is to Lavapiés") because matching operates on texture, not facts.

### § 7 · Match notes (500-800 words, structured)

The structured match data for MESH's matching tool. Each match gets its own paragraph or two:

**Strong matches** — neighborhoods that are genuine analogues. The texture, demographic, daily rhythm, cultural moment all align. A reader who loves this neighborhood will recognize themselves there.

**Adjacent matches** — close but not perfect. Some dimensions align; others diverge. Useful for readers expanding their search.

**Aspirational matches** — neighborhoods this one *resembles past versions of*, or that represent where this one is heading. Honest about gentrification trajectories.

Each match includes 100-300 words of commentary explaining the specific dimensions that align or diverge. **Not "both have good cafés"** — specifically *what kind* of café culture, *what kind* of demographic, *what kind* of relationship to its city.

### § 8 · The friction (200-400 words)

What's genuinely difficult, annoying, or compromised about this neighborhood? Every neighborhood has friction; the profile that pretends otherwise is selling, not analyzing.

Common friction categories: noise, displacement pressure, transit limitations, climate issues, infrastructure gaps, demographic shifts that have changed the character, the gap between what tourists see and what residents experience.

Honest but not snarky. The friction is information, not editorial complaint.

### § 9 · Closing observation (200-400 words)

A short, specific closing thought that doesn't summarize but rather *places* the neighborhood in the broader frame MHYC is building. Connection to other profiles, the studio's broader argument, the cultural moment.

Not "Trinity Bellwoods is vibrant and diverse and you should visit." Something more like "Trinity Bellwoods is what happens when an immigrant working-class neighborhood reaches its third gentrification cycle in 25 years, and the question of what comes next has answers visible on the ground today."

### Closing footer

```
*MHYC.NNNN [Neighborhood Name] profile v[X.Y]. Reviewed [date]. Next scheduled review: [date]. Match corpus updated [date]. Cross-references: [other MHYC profiles linked to this one].*
```

## § 2 · Section length guide

| Section | Target words | Allowed range |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Opening provocation | 300 | 200-400 |
| 2. Current moment | 650 | 500-800 |
| 3. Historical context | 500 | 400-600 |
| 4. Practical math | 650 | 500-800 |
| 5. Community access points | 500 | 400-600 |
| 6. Texture | 500 | 400-600 |
| 7. Match notes | 650 | 500-800 |
| 8. Friction | 300 | 200-400 |
| 9. Closing observation | 300 | 200-400 |
| **Total** | **~4,350** | **3,500-5,000** |

Profiles can exceed 5,000 words for major neighborhoods where the texture warrants it (LES, Tokyo's larger neighborhoods, etc.) but should never fall below 3,500. Anything shorter doesn't have room for the analytical depth that distinguishes MHYC from listicle content.

## § 3 · Editorial standards

### Voice & register

- **Sentences:** precise, opinionated where opinion is earned. Variable rhythm — short hits punctuating longer analytical passages.
- **Authority:** earned, not assumed. Specific observation > general claim.
- **Tone:** analytical with warmth. Not academic-cold. Not lifestyle-warm. **Architectural Review with a heart.**
- **Stance:** willing to take positions. "This restaurant matters" / "This street has become unwalkable" / "The displacement is real and named below."

### Citation & sourcing

- Every number gets a date and a source. Rent ranges, population figures, transit data — link to the underlying source.
- Every named business gets verified (still operating at time of publication).
- Every claim that depends on observation gets framed as observation, not fact.
- Quotes from residents get attributed (with pseudonyms for those who request).

### Photography

- Every profile includes 6-12 photographs. Photographs of structures, streets, public spaces, businesses (with permission for storefront imagery). **Never photographs of identifiable people without explicit consent.**
- Photos are documented: location, date, photographer credit.
- Resolution: web-optimized 1600px wide standard; archive-quality originals kept.

### Updates & revisions

- Annual review cycle: every profile gets revisited each year on its publication anniversary. Most sections stay; § 2 (current moment), § 4 (practical math), § 5 (community access points) update most often.
- Significant changes ship as v1.1, v1.2, etc. The MHYC.NNNN number stays the same.
- Major rewrites (major neighborhood transformations) ship as v2.0 with the old version archived.

### Cross-references

- Every match in § 7 cross-references its corresponding profile if one exists (linked).
- Every profile cross-references at least 2-3 sibling profiles where appropriate (linked).
- Cross-references feed MESH's matching tool corpus.

## § 4 · How to use this template

For Jordan or any future MHYC contributor drafting a new profile:

1. **Copy this template's structure** into a new MHYC.NNNN-[neighborhood-slug].md file
2. **Fill in the header block** with the neighborhood's specific data
3. **Draft each section in order** — they build on each other; § 6 (texture) only works once the foundation is laid
4. **Include `[JORDAN INPUT]` or `[CONTRIBUTOR INPUT]` markers** for sections that need lived-experience knowledge before drafting can complete
5. **Submit for editorial review** before publication. Even a strong draft benefits from a second editorial pass.
